## Fire classes used in the UK and Europe

Fires are classified by what is burning. Class A fires involve solid materials such as wood, paper, cardboard and textiles. Class B fires involve flammable liquids such as petrol, solvents and paints. Class C fires involve flammable gases such as propane, butane and natural gas. Class D fires involve combustible metals such as magnesium, lithium metal and aluminium swarf. Class F fires involve cooking oils and fats. Fires involving live electrical equipment are not a class of their own in the European system, but the electrical risk decides which extinguisher is safe to use.

## Extinguisher types and what they are for

Extinguishers made to BS EN 3 have a red body with a coloured band that identifies the agent. Water (red band) is for Class A only. Foam (cream band) is for Class A and Class B. Carbon dioxide, written CO2 (black band), is for Class B and for fires involving live electrical equipment. Dry powder (blue band) is rated for Class A, B and C and can be used on electrical equipment. Wet chemical (yellow band) is for Class F cooking oil fires and also carries a Class A rating. Class D metal fires need a specialist dry powder made for the metal concerned.

## Selection actions

* Identify what is burning before choosing an extinguisher.
* Use a carbon dioxide extinguisher on live electrical equipment and on small flammable liquid fires.
* Use a wet chemical extinguisher or a fire blanket on a cooking oil or fat fire.
* Use water or foam on ordinary solid materials such as paper, wood and cardboard when no live electrical equipment is involved.
* Use only a specialist Class D powder on burning metal.

## Do not

* Never use water or foam on live electrical equipment, because of the risk of electric shock.
* Never use water on burning cooking oil or fat, because it causes a violent eruption of burning oil.
* Never use a water jet on burning flammable liquid, because it spreads the burning liquid.
* Do not discharge a dry powder extinguisher in a small enclosed room unless there is no alternative: powder cuts visibility and is harmful to breathe, which is why BS 5306 Part 8 advises against specifying powder for general indoor use.

## Note on other countries

The United States system in NFPA 10 differs. There, Class B covers both flammable liquids and gases, Class C means energised electrical equipment and Class K means cooking oils. Always check which system the labels on site follow.
