## Evacuation strategies

Most workplaces use simultaneous evacuation: when the alarm sounds, everyone leaves at once. Tall or complex buildings may use phased evacuation, where the fire floor and the floor above leave first and other floors follow in stages. Hospitals and care homes use progressive horizontal evacuation, moving people through fire doors into the next protected compartment on the same level. The strategy for a building is set by its fire risk assessment and must be known to staff before an emergency.

## Fire warden actions

* Put on the warden tabard or armband so that you can be identified.
* Sweep your assigned area, including toilets, meeting rooms, store rooms and plant areas, and direct everyone to the nearest safe exit.
* Check that people who need assistance are being helped in line with their personal emergency evacuation plan.
* Close doors and windows as you go, if it is safe to do so.
* Leave by the nearest safe exit yourself and report to the assembly point coordinator that your area is clear, or say exactly where it could not be checked.
* Carry out the roll call or sweep report at the assembly point and identify anyone who is unaccounted for.
* Meet the fire and rescue service and tell the officer in charge where the fire is, who is missing and what hazards are present, such as gas cylinders, chemicals or battery stores.

## Do not

* Do not use lifts during a fire unless the lift is a designated evacuation lift under trained control.
* Do not allow anyone to return for belongings or vehicles.
* Do not search an area that is filling with smoke: report it as unchecked instead.
* Do not let people drift away from the assembly point before the roll call is complete.

## Choosing a route

The nearest exit is not always the safest. If smoke or fire lies between people and their usual exit they must turn back and use an alternative route. Escape routes should be kept clear, signed and lit at all times, and final exit doors must open easily without a key.
