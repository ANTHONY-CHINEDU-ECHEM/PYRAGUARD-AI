## Nature of the hazard

A damaged, faulty or overcharged lithium ion cell can enter thermal runaway: it heats itself, vents gas and ignites neighbouring cells in a chain reaction. Warning signs are swelling, hissing or popping, a sweet or chemical smell, unusual heat, and a white or grey vapour cloud. That vapour is both toxic, as it can contain hydrogen fluoride, and flammable, and it can ignite explosively. Battery fires develop in seconds, burn very hot and often reignite hours or even days after they appear to be out.

## Immediate actions

* Raise the alarm and evacuate the area as soon as a battery shows signs of thermal runaway, without waiting for flames.
* Call the fire and rescue service and tell them clearly that lithium ion batteries are involved, with the type and approximate quantity.
* Switch off the charging supply at the isolator if it can be reached safely and away from the battery.
* Close doors on leaving to contain smoke and vapour.
* Keep people well away and upwind of the smoke or vapour cloud.
* Tell the fire and rescue service about any other battery stock or charging equipment nearby.

## Do not

* Do not try to move or carry a battery that is venting, smoking or burning.
* Do not rely on a portable extinguisher to stop thermal runaway: it may knock down flames but the cells can keep reacting and reignite.
* Do not breathe the vapour or smoke.
* Do not return a battery that has been involved in a fire to service or to indoor storage.

## After the event

Fire and rescue services generally use large volumes of water to cool battery fires and limit spread. Batteries that have burned or been damaged should be quarantined outdoors, away from buildings and other combustibles, and collected by a specialist waste contractor. Charging areas should be kept clear of escape routes, separated from combustible storage and covered by automatic detection.
