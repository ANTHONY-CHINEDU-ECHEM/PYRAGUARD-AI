## Why hot spots matter

Many fires give warning before they start. A loose electrical connection, an overloaded cable, a failing motor bearing, a blocked ventilation grille or a battery under charge will run hotter than its surroundings for minutes, hours or days before anything ignites. A thermal camera sees that temperature rise. Acting at this stage is the cheapest and safest point at which to intervene, because nothing is burning yet.

## Actions for a hot spot with no flame or smoke

* Verify the reading on the live thermal view and identify exactly which item of equipment is hot.
* Send a competent person to inspect the equipment, with a carbon dioxide extinguisher to hand and a clear exit.
* Remove the load or switch the equipment off at its isolator if this can be done safely.
* Keep combustible materials away from the hot equipment.
* Continue to monitor the temperature until it has returned to normal.
* Arrange for a qualified electrician or engineer to find and repair the cause before the equipment is used again.
* Treat the event as a fire and raise the alarm if smoke, a smell of burning or a rapidly rising temperature is observed.

## Do not

* Do not touch equipment that is abnormally hot.
* Do not open an enclosure that is hot or smoking.
* Do not put overheated equipment back into service until the cause has been repaired.
* Do not ignore a hot spot because no alarm has sounded.

## Typical thresholds

Alarm temperatures depend on the equipment and should come from the manufacturer's rating. As a general guide, a surface that is tens of degrees above similar equipment under the same load deserves investigation, and a rapidly rising temperature is more significant than a steady one.
